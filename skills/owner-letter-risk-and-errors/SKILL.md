# Owner letter risk and errors

Describe a material adverse event in concrete terms: what happened, when, its observed effect, and the response. Separate a mistake in judgment from an unfavorable outcome that was reasonably possible; call something a mistake only when the author can support that assessment. When relevant, say what was believed at the time, what evidence changed, and what is being done differently.

Discuss uncertainty in terms a reader can use. Name the exposure, the conditions that would worsen or improve it, and what remains unknown. Quantify a range or sensitivity only when it has a sound basis. Avoid vague reassurance, dramatic predictions, and risk lists that obscure the few exposures that matter most.

Make setbacks visible near the results or decisions they qualify, or cross-reference them prominently; do not reduce them to a perfunctory final disclaimer. Give favorable outcomes the same causal discipline by identifying material luck, tax effects, accounting changes, or market movements. Do not invent a confession to make the letter sound candid. If management has not supplied the facts or authorized the characterization, draft a neutral factual account with a question for review.

Candor does not require publishing confidential personnel details, competitively sensitive facts, or unsupported estimates. State the material effect and decision logic as fully as the authorized record permits, and identify the resulting limit for the reader.

Example, fictional: “The new warehouse opened four months late and cost $0.8 million more than budgeted. We underestimated permitting time; the revised project checklist now requires a permit review before contracts are signed.”
